A low pass over planet Earth: the camera flies in low orbit, about three hundred kilometres up, the curved horizon of the planet crossing the frame, the viewer feeling the immense scale of the world spread out below.

THE LOOK: one frame from a top-budget science-fiction film, seen through a virtual camera flying through the solar system. Photoreal, physically based: real light, real materials, real depth, as if the viewer is there. Ultra-wide lens with an immense sense of scale. The Sun is the only key light: hard directional sunlight, a strong rim light, long shadows, deep contrast, with detail held in both the brightest and the darkest areas. Rich, saturated color pushed a little past reality without turning garish. Space is alive: a dense field of pin-sharp stars of varied brightness and tint, the dusty glowing band of the Milky Way crossing the background, faint nebular glow and drifting dust. Extreme fine detail at every scale, layered depth, volumetric haze where there is atmosphere. Natural lens bloom on the brightest highlights. It must not look like a spacecraft photograph, a textbook illustration, a painting or a stylized render, and nothing may look flat, plastic or plain. No text, labels, borders, watermarks, interface elements, spacecraft or people. 16:9 landscape.

THE SHOT: looking sideways along the coast with the camera banked; the horizon is a gentle curve running diagonally across the upper part of the frame from left to upper right, black space above it; the Sun is off-frame to the left.

SCALE (critical, true physical scale for a camera three hundred kilometres up): the ground below is seen from very high above, like a vast relief map. The glowing blue atmosphere band on the horizon is the thickest thing standing above the surface. Thunderheads are about fifteen kilometres tall, so from here they are small: each storm tower is a compact, brilliant white cauliflower knot with a flat anvil spreading around it, only a few percent of the frame wide, its height far less than the thickness of the blue atmosphere band, and it never rises toward the horizon line. Their grandeur comes from their number, their fine detail and the long shadows they cast, not from their height. Clusters of them recede in perspective into a fine texture toward the horizon.

THE SURFACE: deep sapphire ocean with a real, recognizable coastline: the southern coast of Italy, Sicily and the Strait of Messina, with Mount Etna and the Apennines, the deep blue Mediterranean, and the turquoise shallows of the Gulf of Gabes in the distance. Turquoise and jade shallows over sand banks and reefs, white surf lines, river mouths spilling silt plumes into the sea, green-gold land with real terrain colour. Islands, reefs and land lie flat at sea level and cast no shadows; only clouds cast shadows. A blinding sun glint blazes on the water, a broad molten white-gold path across the sea in which current lines, wind slicks and wave fields show as fine silver texture. The thunderhead clusters stand over the sea and coast, sunward sides blazing white-gold, far sides cool blue, each casting a long, thin black shadow streaming across the sea and the lower cloud field, every shadow pointing directly away from the Sun. Fields of tiny popcorn cumulus and thin cirrus streaks add layered depth. All cloud is soft vapour: no pits, no dimples, no hard-rimmed spots.

THE HORIZON: the curved limb of the Earth, with the atmosphere glowing electric blue in thin layered shells, from a bright white-cyan line at the horizon through deep cobalt into black space. Above it, deep black space; because the camera is exposed for the sunlit planet, only a sparse few faint pin-sharp stars show, well away from the Sun's direction, and no Milky Way.

LIGHT: one hard, distant Sun off-frame to the left and low, cross-lighting the scene from the side, so each storm cluster is lit on its left and throws a long shadow to the right; the sun glint sits on the sea in the left part of the frame. Low sun, so every cloud edge is rim-lit and every shadow is long. Everything in sharp focus from foreground to the horizon: no depth-of-field blur, no bokeh, no floating debris.

CAMERA AND GRADE: photographed like a large-format film frame from a top-budget science-fiction film, through an anamorphic lens, wide. A soft warm veil of flare and one horizontal anamorphic streak tied to the brightest point of the sun glint on the water. Cool blue-black shadows against warm gold highlights, very fine film grain, gentle natural vignette. Low-key and high contrast. No spacecraft, no station hardware, no window frame, no solar panels, nothing in the foreground but the planet.
