Mars, a film frame from a low flight over Valles Marineris just as dawn breaks.

THE LOOK: one frame from a top-budget science-fiction film, seen through a virtual camera flying through the solar system. Photoreal, physically based: real light, real materials, real depth, as if the viewer is there. Ultra-wide lens with an immense sense of scale. The Sun is the only key light: hard directional sunlight, a strong rim light, long shadows, deep contrast, with detail held in both the brightest and the darkest areas. Rich, saturated color pushed a little past reality without turning garish. Space is alive: a dense field of pin-sharp stars of varied brightness and tint, the dusty glowing band of the Milky Way crossing the background, faint nebular glow and drifting dust. Extreme fine detail at every scale, layered depth, volumetric haze where there is atmosphere. Natural lens bloom on the brightest highlights. It must not look like a spacecraft photograph, a textbook illustration, a painting or a stylized render, and nothing may look flat, plastic or plain. No text, labels, borders, watermarks, interface elements, spacecraft or people. 16:9 landscape.

REFERENCE IMAGES: Image 1 is a different planet, Saturn, and is for color grade, contrast, lens character, star field density and rendering quality only. Never copy Saturn's rings, globe, colors, moons, Titan, or composition. Image 2 is the actual Mars Valles Marineris terrain reference; use its paired fault scarps, fluted wall gullies, landslide aprons, width and scale only. Image 3 shows Mars itself; use the dry surface features and rust coloration as subject identifiers. Never copy the daylight, camera view or documentary look of either mission image.

This is the surface of Mars, in first-person flight over the rim of Melas Chasma, part of Valles Marineris. Convey the reason this canyon isn't a canyon on Earth: a startlingly broad complex of dry linked fault troughs, dozens of huge layers and ridges, and mountains of old rockslides banked against the walls. Miles of iron-red terrain, several distinct flat parallel grabens, hard shale and basalt shelves, rust-colored dust, not painterly. The near escarpment is huge but the camera is still kilometers above it, with large terrain features and scree fields resolved but no fist-sized pebbles. Past those walls and as part of the same unbroken topography, a natural horizon runs across the frame, only subtly convex, with barely perceptible curvature near the edges. Terrain and air meet organically: receding distant ridgelines simply get lower, finer and slightly paler until they are lost in a very thin butterscotch atmospheric rim, not a wide glowing stripe or an elevated planar disk.

CAMERA: Every foreground plane should feel in motion. Fly along the edge of a massive scalloped terracotta escarpment running diagonally mid-right to lower-left, sweep past deep shadowed scars with subtle reflected light. Looking forward-left, show the floor under intermittently lit patches of thin gray-ochre ground fog and give the broad shadowed far rim an amber outline, coherent with the off-frame Sun just left. No artificial high contrast breaks at the horizon; a low gentle convex profile in the uppermost quarter above the real highlands.

LIGHT AND ATMOSPHERE: One hard Sun is low and off-screen. Never draw the Sun itself, ramps of flare, visible sunshafts, or unnatural glow. Raking sunlight covers little of the image: only selected high ridges and outward-facing rim edges catch pools of amber while coherent long shadows fall down and away from the light. The canyon interior stays cool, deep and very detailed; very soft scattered illumination in the shade preserves dark burgundy layers and tonal relief. Delicate ground mist settles as irregular low sheets on the floor with visible clear holes and mountainside bases emerging; do not use brightly lit beams inside the fog or soften the cliffs. The scene has a clear gradation from warmer thin gold at the rim to deep cool sky with a low-contrast vast star field only at the uppermost edge. Stars are fine and dark; the Milky Way a nearly invisible lane, never luminous. Expose for the canyon. With a visible but very quiet atmospheric line, no part of sky can compete with the extraordinary stratigraphy. Fine optical grain and no depth-of-field blur.
