Mars with foreground Phobos: both complete and visible, a 16:9 cinematic whole-world orbital view.

THE LOOK: one frame from a top-budget science-fiction film, seen through a virtual camera flying through the solar system. Photoreal, physically based: real light, real materials, real depth, as if the viewer is there. Ultra-wide lens with an immense sense of scale. The Sun is the only key light: hard directional sunlight, a strong rim light, long shadows, deep contrast, with detail held in both the brightest and the darkest areas. Rich, saturated color pushed a little past reality without turning garish. Space is alive: a dense field of pin-sharp stars of varied brightness and tint, the dusty glowing band of the Milky Way crossing the background, faint nebular glow and drifting dust. Extreme fine detail at every scale, layered depth, volumetric haze where there is atmosphere. Natural lens bloom on the brightest highlights. It must not look like a spacecraft photograph, a textbook illustration, a painting or a stylized render, and nothing may look flat, plastic or plain. No text, labels, borders, watermarks, interface elements, spacecraft or people. 16:9 landscape.

LAYOUT AND REFERENCE ROLES: Image 1 is the exact Mars preview: preserve geometry, all framing, exposure split and camera crop; re-render at film finish. The globe, including its deliberately dark left side, is entirely ON SCREEN, with at least 6% of the frame height of black above and below its limb. Do not crop, zoom, or recenter the globe; the dark limb must never go off frame. Keep the relative size and positions of the preview's Mars and Phobos. Image 2 is a different planet, Saturn, for grade, lens character, dark star density, contrast and the film finish only. Never copy Saturn's globe, rings, Titan, moons, colors or composition. Image 3 is real Martian dust for its low regional pattern only, not for composition, monochrome colors, or the look. The preview itself already uses NASA's perfectly recognizable Mars terrain.

SUBJECT AND WEATHER: Mars shows a whole entire opaque sphere with believable geography at globe scale: bright white polar cap on the upper sunlit shoulder; Valles Marineris a long, interrelated dark corridor of fitted dry rifts and side gullies running across low latitudes, grouped broad Tharsis volcanic shields and summit pits west of it. All geography must be naturally low, sharp, not a white embossed crack or a plain smooth sphere. A localized Martian dust storm unfurls low across a sunlit basin below the end of one valley, completely inside the disc and far from the terminator. It is broad, translucent, slightly lighter than the dark russet plain and obviously windswept, a comma-like scalloped wall of windblown grains spilling around the ground following the shape of the basin, not a cloud object, not a fire or cyclone, no eye or circular swirl. It has negligible height relative to very wide footprint. Some craters are visible through its lightly strewn tails. Composition is exciting through natural surface depth and precise light, not exaggerating the dust.

CAMERA: High-angle view over the whole planet banked gently face-on to the pole with the all-on-screen rim as an enormous frame accent and Phobos small at upper right. No empty-stock balance: the exact tilt and directional light should guide the eye across the ice, then the shadows of the volcanoes, then the remote near-surface rust dust front. Relative amplitudes, scale and relief real. Stars dim and crisp with no large bright background swirls.

The visual intention is flying past Phobos and diving toward one side of the blood-red world. Achieve deep fore/middle/background layers: charcoal gray Phobos, enormous red Mars, distant stars. Phobos's realistic egg-off-center shape, dim dusty regolith, one larger crisp bowl with a ring of much smaller pocks and coherent natural sharpness. No drop shadow or halo. Right-side grazing light from a single off-frame Sun illuminates both bodies; the sharp relief on the side of that one tiny moon and the planet's low terrain relief must agree, and there is no on-screen Sun or flare streak. Soft little volumetric scatter precisely on the sunlit limb, not a painted neon line. Fine grain, gentle vignette and dark-burgundy albedo among the lit burnt-red terrains. The dense space stars are sharp, tiny and far dimmer than any sunlight. Milky Way's faint visual texture is the size of a distant sky, not a bright streak centered behind the moon.
