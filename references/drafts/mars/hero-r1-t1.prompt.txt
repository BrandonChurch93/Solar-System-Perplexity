Mars, the whole world from nearby orbit, recognizable immediately, large and dominant in the frame.

THE LOOK: one frame from a top-budget science-fiction film, seen through a virtual camera flying through the solar system. Photoreal, physically based: real light, real materials, real depth, as if the viewer is there. Ultra-wide lens with an immense sense of scale. The Sun is the only key light: hard directional sunlight, a strong rim light, long shadows, deep contrast, with detail held in both the brightest and the darkest areas. Rich, saturated color pushed a little past reality without turning garish. Space is alive: a dense field of pin-sharp stars of varied brightness and tint, the dusty glowing band of the Milky Way crossing the background, faint nebular glow and drifting dust. Extreme fine detail at every scale, layered depth, volumetric haze where there is atmosphere. Natural lens bloom on the brightest highlights. It must not look like a spacecraft photograph, a textbook illustration, a painting or a stylized render, and nothing may look flat, plastic or plain. No text, labels, borders, watermarks, interface elements, spacecraft or people. 16:9 landscape.

Full Mars, every edge of the disc inside the image, overwhelmingly dominant and filling nearly nine-tenths of the frame height. The world is dry and geologically tactile: iron oxides across deep rust, ochre and accents of crimson; fine crater ejecta, lava plains and dune streaks. The sprawling Valles Marineris system is the first feature that catches the eye, a dark, complex band of broad, discontinuous, stepped and parallel fault canyons across the low latitudes, not one clean crack. Immediately to its west, catch long sunlight over the wide, low Tharsis shield volcanoes and their inset calderas. Keep one irregularly scalloped bright-white polar cap on the lit side and curl one localized ochre dust front across a separate basin with fingered lobes and visible ground beneath the edges, never a planet-wide cloud blanket. Round the sunlit limb with an optically thin amber glint resolving into vacuum. Phobos is visibly closer to camera but small relative to Mars; rough low-albedo charcoal, irregular potato-like silhouette, Stickney depression and tiny grooves partly revealed by the same raking sun. No second moon.

CAMERA AND LIGHT: Camera three-quarter sunward with the full sphere slightly right of center, raking light from high left. Valles angles down across the forward face; hold real low contrast detail through the transition into darkness. Phobos crosses a lower piece of lit open terrain, away from the central canyon complex.

EXPOSURE: one off-frame Sun, no additional light sources, no flare streaks. Keep very soft contact cues inside the angular rock relief, cool ebony shadows with detail and warmly illuminated russet highlights, deep contrast at the planet's scale. Dense but subtle pin-sharp stars are all in black open sky and faint beside any sunlit rock, a dusky limited Milky Way lane well behind the globe. Both Mars and Phobos are solid and opaque, completely blocking stars on their silhouettes. Everything is tack-sharp, with very fine photochemical grain and a soft natural edge falloff.
