Mars and foreground Phobos seen in a fully composed whole-world orbital shot.

THE LOOK: one frame from a top-budget science-fiction film, seen through a virtual camera flying through the solar system. Photoreal, physically based: real light, real materials, real depth, as if the viewer is there. Ultra-wide lens with an immense sense of scale. The Sun is the only key light: hard directional sunlight, a strong rim light, long shadows, deep contrast, with detail held in both the brightest and the darkest areas. Rich, saturated color pushed a little past reality without turning garish. Space is alive: a dense field of pin-sharp stars of varied brightness and tint, the dusty glowing band of the Milky Way crossing the background, faint nebular glow and drifting dust. Extreme fine detail at every scale, layered depth, volumetric haze where there is atmosphere. Natural lens bloom on the brightest highlights. It must not look like a spacecraft photograph, a textbook illustration, a painting or a stylized render, and nothing may look flat, plastic or plain. No text, labels, borders, watermarks, interface elements, spacecraft or people. 16:9 landscape.

Image 1 is the exact geometric layout. Keep geometry and re-render at film finish: one perfectly round opaque Mars, single Phobos, solar angle, both sizes, positions and phases. Replace layout plain paint and sky. The little dark point is Phobos: make it look like physically modeled irregular, battered dark basalt with individually resolved tiny craters and an oblong silhouette, but its diameter exactly as laid out, only 2% of the frame width, with unlit parts completely dark and no halo or drop shadow. Image 2 is Saturn and is ONLY for photographic color grade, contrast, lens, density of faint stars and rendering quality. Do not copy Saturn's globe, rings, Titan, colors, moons, or composition. Image 3 is the real Mars subject montage for the geology and the identity of the planet. Image 4 is a real photograph of a Martian dust storm for texture and footprint only. Do not borrow the look, grade, light or camera of those last two mission images.

Full Mars, huge foreground world, different from the cold light of the sky: matte deep rust, terracotta, ochre and crimson, with the long triple-trenched scar of Valles Marineris unmistakable in angular raking light, Tharsis a chain of wide low shields with sharp dark summit calderas, and hard white frost at the pole. Dust event is a focal story beat: show an unambiguously visible broad crescent-shaped dust storm turning across the rust-and-charcoal lower latitudes, just to the side underneath the sunlit Valles end, occupying a patch about one-eighth of the illuminated face. Its backward-curving front is visibly warmer and brighter than the craters that show through it, finely feathered, flat against the ground and driven sideways by wind, feather-striated like high quality aerial cinematography of a haboob; not puffy fire smoke, not atmospheric bands. Put it away from the terminator so it cannot look like a hovering attachment. Leave geography around it in beautiful clean view: shape the enormous canyon as one relief feature, not the edge of the dust. Fix the Mars limb well-defined on both sides, one thin diffused amber atmosphere line and no excessive glow.

CAMERA AND COMPOSITION: Approach the very near dark Phobos in the lower left, with Mars loose at frame right looming beautifully and its entire globe intact. Frame Phobos physically tiny, no more than 2% frame width even in close foreground. The hard off-camera upper-left key puts its darkness on the right, matching the globe. Flow a fingerprint of winding dust around a sunlit highland west and above the great canyon, a bold localized cream-orange curl against crisp craters and the surrounding crimson rock; leave Tharsis west of the canyon but north of the dust, with obvious low mountain profiles.

FINISH: Tone: clearly visually directed rather than explanatory. Film-light Mars from only one distant off-screen side, so caldera ramps and relief rise and fall in drama rather than showing every landmark in flat view. Phase of Mars and the distant Sun’s matching grazing light on foreground Phobos must agree precisely; preserve deep cool shadows, entirely matte texture, and dark floating openness of the new Martian composition. Optical depth and restrained frame falloff, very fine analogue grain. Dense pin-sharp stars, the Milky Way as a very distant faint strip of dim dust that never approaches the surface brightness, without rings, edge halos or glare lines. No star can appear inside a solid object’s silhouette.
