THE GLOBE: unmistakably Saturn, not Jupiter: a vast, serene, hazy golden world of gas. Dozens of parallel bands in butterscotch, honey, straw, champagne and cream under a pearly upper haze, shading to cool blue-grey at the north pole with the six-sided polar hexagon. Within that calm order, detail no telescope has ever resolved: long silk-fine filaments and ripples along every band edge, rows of tiny scalloped waves, soft curling eddies, and stacked haze layers whose long shadows at the terminator give the cloud deck real depth. It is all soft vapour: no pockmarks, no crater-like dimples, no hard-rimmed spots, no heavy brown belts, no red spot. A thin luminous atmosphere line glows gold-white along the sunlit limb. The night side is not black: it is faintly lit by warm light reflected from the rings.

THE RINGS: thousands of razor-fine concentric bands of water ice in champagne, pale gold, rose-grey, pearl and silver, no two alike, crisp to the far edge: the dusky, translucent inner C ring, the dense brilliant B ring, the broad dark Cassini Division as a clear near-black gap, the A ring with the hair-thin Encke gap, and the thread of the F ring outside. The bright B and A rings are dense and opaque: no stars show through them, and no stars show inside the globe's shadow on the rings, which is simply dark ring. Nearest the camera the bands resolve into their true texture, a fine matte grain of countless ice particles, with sunlight scattering softly through the ice so the bright rings glow gently from within. Everything in sharp focus from foreground to far distance: no depth-of-field blur, no bokeh, no floating debris.
