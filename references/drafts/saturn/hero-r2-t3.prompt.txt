Saturn, the whole planet inside its full ring system, large and dominant in the frame, composed like a film poster.

THE LOOK: one frame from a top-budget science-fiction film, seen through a virtual camera flying through the solar system. Photoreal, physically based: real light, real materials, real depth, as if the viewer is there. Ultra-wide lens with an immense sense of scale. The Sun is the only key light: hard directional sunlight, a strong rim light, long shadows, deep contrast, with detail held in both the brightest and the darkest areas. Rich, saturated color pushed a little past reality without turning garish. Space is alive: a dense field of pin-sharp stars of varied brightness and tint, the dusty glowing band of the Milky Way crossing the background, faint nebular glow and drifting dust. Extreme fine detail at every scale, layered depth, volumetric haze where there is atmosphere. Natural lens bloom on the brightest highlights. It must not look like a spacecraft photograph, a textbook illustration, a painting or a stylized render, and nothing may look flat, plastic or plain. No text, labels, borders, watermarks, interface elements, spacecraft or people. 16:9 landscape.

COMPOSITION: a low, sweeping view from only about 10 degrees above the ring plane, close over the outer rings. The rings rush toward the camera as a vast gleaming plain filling the bottom third of the frame and wrapping around the globe; beyond the globe the far side of the rings rises as a thin bright ellipse. The whole globe stands centre-left above the ring plain, about 60 percent of the frame height, like a golden world rising over a frozen sea.

THE GLOBE: unmistakably Saturn and not Jupiter: a serene, hazy, pale-gold world. Dozens of soft parallel bands in butterscotch, straw, champagne and cream, stepping gently in tone under a pearly upper haze, shading to cool blue-grey at the north pole where the six-sided polar hexagon is just visible. The detail is fine and delicate rather than violent: thin wavy filaments and ripples along the band edges, faint streaks, a few small pale oval storms, soft cloud shadows that give the deck real depth. No heavy brown belts, no large swirling turbulence, no red spot. A thin luminous atmosphere line glows along the sunlit limb.

THE RINGS: solid-looking only from afar; here they resolve into thousands of razor-fine concentric bands of water ice in champagne, pale gold, rose-grey and silver, no two alike, with the broad dark Cassini Division as a clear near-black gap between the bright B ring and the A ring, and the thin Encke gap near the outer edge. Stars show faintly through the thinner rings. Where the rings come close to the camera the bands break up into countless tiny grains and chunks of ice, each catching a pinpoint of sunlight like frost at sunrise, with light scattering through the ice so the rings glow from within.

LIGHT: the Sun is out of frame to the right, low over the ring plane, so its light rakes across the rings and makes the near ice glitter. The right two thirds of the globe is lit in warm gold with a brilliant rim on the right limb; the left third falls through a soft terminator into deep shadow, faintly warmed by light reflected off the rings. The rings cast a tight set of fine dark parallel shadow lines across the globe. The globe casts one long, hard-edged black shadow across the rings on the left, pointing directly away from the Sun. The globe is completely opaque: nothing behind it shows through. All shadows agree with that one Sun direction.

DISTANCE: Titan hangs small in the upper left distance, a hazy orange sphere with a soft fuzzy atmosphere edge, lit from the right. Behind everything the dusty band of the Milky Way arcs across the upper frame among dense, sharp stars.
