Mercury, unmistakably the innermost world: agile cinematic flight over crater rims and cliffs at the terminator. 16:9.

THE LOOK: one frame from a top-budget science-fiction film, seen through a virtual camera flying through the solar system. Photoreal, physically based: real light, real materials, real depth, as if the viewer is there. Ultra-wide lens with an immense sense of scale. The Sun is the only key light: hard directional sunlight, a strong rim light, long shadows, deep contrast, with detail held in both the brightest and the darkest areas. Rich, saturated color pushed a little past reality without turning garish. Space is alive: a dense field of pin-sharp stars of varied brightness and tint, the dusty glowing band of the Milky Way crossing the background, faint nebular glow and drifting dust. Extreme fine detail at every scale, layered depth, volumetric haze where there is atmosphere. Natural lens bloom on the brightest highlights. It must not look like a spacecraft photograph, a textbook illustration, a painting or a stylized render, and nothing may look flat, plastic or plain. No text, labels, borders, watermarks, interface elements, spacecraft or people. 16:9 landscape.

TERRAIN: an extremely low orbital shot on Mercury, not a ground-level macro shot. Render the hard, exposed, relentlessly cratered vacuum terrain. We clear the very top of a kilometer-high terrace on one giant oval impact scar; visible near terrain at least two kilometers away, everything is in equally tack-sharp focus with no macro, no depth-of-field blur or round bokeh. Flashing sharp mineral flakes are sparse physically tiny details in the actual rock, not beads, floating points, or overly white flecks. Recognizable dark grey, charcoal and bronze terrain whose brooding neutrals are interrupted by one fresh Kuiper-style ray crater casting very fine pale branching ejecta over ancient darker surface. The nearby dark basin includes Mercury's unique scalloped shallow blue-grey hollows (Ramshackle irregular flat-floored pits in tight clusters scalloping the upper wall around the central peaks, not blue light, not lava). An enormous contraction scarp cuts one old crater across its diameter and continues on toward a second cliff, not angular Earth rock layers. Bold real physical depth. An uninterrupted hard shallow arc of world curvature in the upper third against stars, no haze or any atmospheric rim.

FRAMING: 21 mm, fairly level camera on a left bank following a lobate scarp almost edge-on. Sun at far upper right, only a narrow section of its huge arc protrudes from behind the hard kink of the horizon. The foreground and central cliff below the camera absorb all the blackness, while sun catches the opposite cliff, a narrow ledge and a far crater, making several blade-like silhouettes. The pale cluster of shallower hollows sits near center in an exposed wall, with just a hint of reflected sunlight.

LIGHT AND CAMERA: one large solar photosphere just beside the hard horizon, noticeably larger than Earth's Sun and the only light. Part of its arc is blocked by actual dark rock on the hard horizon. Keep the rest of the terrain on the night side with only the upper rims and ridge tops in intense warm-white sunlight. Use a truly low sunlight angle so the steep cliff shadows run many kilometers, aligned away from the sole light. Cool charcoal shadows, near-neutral slate refracted mineral flecks, pale bronze sunlit stone, deliberate low-key film exposure. In far space opposite the Sun only, pin-sharp faint stars and a near-invisible Milky Way, no stars near glare, no atmospheric sunrise gradient, no white fluffy matter. Dark foreground to bright landscapes, lens veiling glare sharply localized to the source.
