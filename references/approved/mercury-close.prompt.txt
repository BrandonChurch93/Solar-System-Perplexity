Mercury from inside a vehicle-free first-person flight, skimming the surface at the terminator.

THE LOOK: one frame from a top-budget science-fiction film, seen through a virtual camera flying through the solar system. Photoreal, physically based: real light, real materials, real depth, as if the viewer is there. Ultra-wide lens with an immense sense of scale. The Sun is the only key light: hard directional sunlight, a strong rim light, long shadows, deep contrast, with detail held in both the brightest and the darkest areas. Rich, saturated color pushed a little past reality without turning garish. Space is alive: a dense field of pin-sharp stars of varied brightness and tint, the dusty glowing band of the Milky Way crossing the background, faint nebular glow and drifting dust. Extreme fine detail at every scale, layered depth, volumetric haze where there is atmosphere. Natural lens bloom on the brightest highlights. It must not look like a spacecraft photograph, a textbook illustration, a painting or a stylized render, and nothing may look flat, plastic or plain. No text, labels, borders, watermarks, interface elements, spacecraft or people. 16:9 landscape.

TERRAIN: low forward flight across the Mercurian terminator, not a top-down survey. A single huge impact crater sweeps toward and underneath camera, with authentic nested impact morphology, shattered kilometer-high cliffs and slumped stepped walls. One giant curving Mercury-style lobate scarp cuts a crater cleanly, alongside the grooves, fractured blocks, rockslide tongues and sharp secondary circular pits of an ancient airless world. Dark crater floor and nearer sunlit ledges in one composition, with finer regolith peppering the flatter areas. White-grey crushed ejecta from a fresh ray crater trails naturally along the plains. Mineral flecks and tiny shards of impact glass snap into view as extremely sparse mirror pinpoints embedded in just the near sunlit rock: hard neutral grey, warm bronze in grading, charcoal black in shadow. Huge world-scale parallax in near rim, mid-distance rim, distant far rim and far crater fields. Horizon is a single bare solid convex arc of rock across the frame; its curvature must be unmistakable, with an airless hard edge against the dark stars. One grazing light from just beyond frame at under two degrees above the local horizon lets only the top ridges catch light. The black shadows are long, crisp, and physically consistent with this one Sun.

FRAMING: 20 mm immersive lens passing lengthwise along a Mercurian lobate scarp, with the planet sloping from an enormous foreground impact bowl at right into a level fracture plain at left; two farther concentric crater rims are clear by daylight and a young ray crater sprays faint pale threads across the smooth rock. The bowed horizon spans the upper half. Single low Sun immediately offscreen top-left; razor-long shadows stretch right across the whole terrain. Near ridge lit along its crown, the sheer wall facing the camera has individual cooled mineral glints, and most deep areas are cool black.
